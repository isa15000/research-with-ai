Research: Spatial Epidemiology and Health Equity

Integrating Geography, Environment, and Disease Data Across Underrepresented Communities

Research Question

How can we more effectively map and understand disease patterns by integrating geographical data on access to healthcare resources, environmental factors, and socioeconomic conditions—while centering the experiences and health outcomes of minority groups and underrepresented communities often overlooked in traditional epidemiological research?

Why This Research Matters

Health disparities are not random; they are shaped by geography, environment, and access to resources. Communities with limited access to healthcare, clean water, nutritious food, and employment opportunities experience higher rates of chronic disease, higher mortality, and worse health outcomes. Yet, these patterns often remain invisible in aggregate national statistics that mask the lived experiences of minority and underrepresented populations.

By merging spatial data on environmental conditions with disease occurrence and treatment outcomes, we can:

  • Identify health disparities at granular geographical scales (neighborhoods, counties, regions)
  • Uncover causal relationships between environmental factors (pollution, food deserts, transportation barriers) and disease burden
  • Highlight how structural inequities concentrate disease risk in specific communities
  • Inform targeted, equitable health interventions and resource allocation
  • Amplify the voices and stories of underrepresented populations through data

Integrating Spatial and Medical Data: Approaches and Directions

1. Mapping Environmental Risk and Disease Burden

Combining geographic information systems (GIS) with epidemiological data reveals the spatial relationship between environmental hazards—such as industrial pollution, urban heat islands, or pesticide exposure—and disease clusters. This approach can expose how minority communities disproportionately live near environmental hazards.

2. Healthcare Access and Social Determinants

Incorporating data on the proximity to hospitals, specialists, pharmacies, and preventive care services alongside demographic and health outcome data reveals "healthcare deserts." Understanding these barriers is essential for understanding why certain communities experience worse health outcomes independent of disease incidence.

3. Centering Underrepresented Populations

Traditional health datasets often exclude or underrepresent minority groups, immigrants, low-income communities, and other marginalized populations. By intentionally collecting and integrating data from these communities—through community health workers, participatory research, and trusted local institutions—we create a more complete and equitable picture of health in America.

Useful Resources and Next Steps

To dive deeper into this research area, consider exploring the following resources:

  • Spatial Epidemiology Tools: QGIS, ArcGIS, and R packages (sf, sp, tmap) enable geographic mapping of health data
  • Public Datasets: CDC Wonder database, EPA environmental data, Census demographic and economic data
  • Community-Based Data: Participatory epidemiology projects and community health surveys
  • Methodological Frameworks: Social determinants of health (SDOH) models and health equity assessment tools
  • Case Studies: Research on environmental justice, redlining's health impacts, and health outcomes in food deserts
