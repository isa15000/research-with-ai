Research question

How does postpartum healthcare utilization vary across Washington State, particularly across geographic and insurance groups?

Healthcare utilization means using healthcare services. Here, the primary measure is whether a respondent received a maternal postpartum checkup. A checkup measures one aspect of utilization; it does not capture all access to care or its quality.

What patterns may suggest

Questions for investigation

01

Descriptive comparisons

Geographic and insurance patterns are descriptive. Differences between groups do not establish that insurance coverage or geographic location causes differences in postpartum healthcare utilization. Other factors, including provider availability, transportation, income, health needs, and other barriers to care, may contribute.

02

Keep the measures distinct

The 91% PRAMS indicator measures people receiving a visit; the CMS series measures billed services relative to all female Medicaid and CHIP beneficiaries ages 15–44. They provide complementary context but are not directly comparable.

03

A documented disruption

The Medicaid service rate drops sharply in April 2020, consistent with pandemic-era disruption. Later values recover but remain below most 2018–2019 months. This describes service volume, not individual continuity or quality of care.

Data & methods

A descriptive, exploratory study

Washington’s Title V action plan supplies the 2023 PRAMS benchmark. CMS T-MSIS data supply monthly Medicaid and CHIP postpartum-service rates for 2018–2022. The local Perinatal Dashboard workbook contains no ACH-level maternal postpartum-checkup measure.

Population and primary outcome

People who recently had a live birth and participated in PRAMS. The requested outcome is “Had maternal postpartum checkup.” Confirm the exact question, denominator, recall window, and survey weighting in the supplied source before adding estimates.

Geography, insurance, and years

The county map represents 2024 Maternity Support Services utilization among people with Medicaid-paid perinatal care. The separate PRAMS benchmark is statewide for 2023, and the CMS trend is statewide for 2018–2022.

Mapped measure

The county percentage is derived from HCA’s published counts: clients receiving MSS both before and after delivery plus clients receiving MSS only after delivery, divided by all individuals with Medicaid-paid perinatal care. Counties with any required suppressed component are not calculated. MSS includes preventive health and education services and is not equivalent to a routine postpartum medical checkup.

Estimates and uncertainty

PRAMS percentages are survey estimates where applicable. Display source-provided 95% confidence intervals and reliability notes. Missing and suppressed observations remain unavailable, never zero. Correlation and group differences do not establish causation.

Medicaid service series

CMS reports monthly postpartum visits and rates per 1,000 female Medicaid and CHIP beneficiaries ages 15–44. This claims-based service rate is not a percentage of postpartum respondents and has no private-insurance comparison. CMS flags unusable cells as DQ and small cells as DS; none of the selected Washington rows carried those flags.

Data still needed

ACH-level PRAMS postpartum-visit estimates and a comparable outcome by insurance group are still needed to answer the geographic and insurance comparison directly. Include weighted percentages, confidence intervals, suppression notes, period, and coverage timing. See the data requirements.

Limitations

What a checkup measure can tell us

Receiving a postpartum checkup is a measure of healthcare utilization and does not capture every dimension of healthcare access or quality. Observational, descriptive comparisons cannot establish causation.

PRAMS responses are self-reported. Survey estimates contain sampling uncertainty; small geographic samples may yield unreliable or suppressed estimates. Broad regions may hide differences within them.

Insurance and geography may not be available at identical levels. The current data gap prevents estimating geographic or insurance differences in checkup utilization.
